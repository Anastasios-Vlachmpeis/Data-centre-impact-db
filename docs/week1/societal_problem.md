## The challenge

Hyperscale data centres bring benefits that are global or national: computing power for AI, corporate profit and economic growth. Their costs are almost entirely local: higher electricity bills when grid expansion is paid for by all customers, large water use for cooling, constant noise, fossil-fuelled backup generators, and very few permanent jobs once construction ends. The conflict is also about procedure. Incentives are set years in advance, zoning often allows data centres "by right", and water use is often not disclosed, so residents have little say. The result is opposition across political lines, and it is starting to slow down the AI build-out. In short: people agree data centres are useful, but they do not want one in their own backyard.

## Why we are interested

Our degree is about AI, but we have barely discussed the infrastructure that makes AI work, or what its expansion does to society. Most of the build-out is in the US, but Europe needs its own AI capacity too, so the same issue applies to the Netherlands.

## Stakeholders

- **Local residents**: bills, noise, water, property values
- **Municipal councils and planners**: tax revenue vs. what residents want
- **Energy regulators and national government**: grid reliability, how tariffs are set
- **Utilities and grid operators**: who pays for the grid, risk of unused assets
- **Hyperscalers, developers and investors**: permits, cost of delays
- **AI users in general**
- **Other water and land users**: farms, golf courses and others
